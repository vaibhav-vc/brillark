# Disconfirmation report — 8 discovery interviews

- **Skill:** disconfirmation-reporting
- **Author agent:** customer-discovery-interviewer
- **Date:** 2026-09-14
- **Confidence:** estimated

## Summary

**Sample: 8.** Two of eight (25%) already solve this with a spreadsheet and explicitly said they
would not switch — that finding leads, per procedure, because it is disconfirming and disconfirming
evidence is reported first. The six who "liked the idea" are not validation: liking an idea is a
stated-intent signal, not a behavioural one, and none of the six were asked about a specific past
instance of the problem or what, if anything, they currently pay or do about it. This sample cannot
support a go decision as it stands.

## Body

### What disconfirmed the hypothesis (2 of 8)

Two participants already have a working solution: a spreadsheet. Two things make this significant
rather than a minor objection:

1. **A workaround already exists and is in active use.** This is the strongest form of evidence
   against demand for a paid alternative — not absence of a problem, but presence of an accepted,
   free solution. Per the anti-pattern this skill guards against, treating an existing workaround as
   a minor objection rather than as evidence is how organisations talk themselves past a real signal.
2. **Both stated they would not switch**, not merely that they were satisfied. "Would not switch" is
   closer to a behavioural signal than the six positive responses below, because it describes a
   decision they have effectively already made (staying with the spreadsheet) rather than a decision
   they are speculating about.

This is a **partial disconfirmation**, not fatal: 2 of 8 is not a majority, but at 25% of a small
sample it is large enough that it should not be waved through as noise, and the underlying reason
(a free, adequate workaround) is exactly the kind of finding that tends to be present in the
un-interviewed population too, not just these two.

### What did not confirm the hypothesis (6 of 8)

Six participants "liked the idea." This requires two distinctions before it counts as evidence:

- **Stated enthusiasm is not evidence of purchase behaviour.** None of the six were reported as
  describing a specific past instance where they paid for, built, or actively sought a solution to
  this problem. "I like the idea" is the response non-buyers give as readily as buyers.
- **We do not know if any of the six also have a workaround.** If some fraction of the six are
  quietly also spreadsheet users who are simply being polite about a hypothetical product, the real
  disconfirmation rate is higher than 25%. This is an open question, not an assumption either way.

Liking the idea is not counted as validation here. It is recorded as a data point, not a result.

### Reading the sample honestly

At n=8, no pattern here should be treated as more than directional. What can be said: a real,
already-adopted, free alternative was found in this small sample, which is the single most useful
finding of the round, because it tells you exactly who the actual competitor is (the spreadsheet,
not another startup) and what your value proposition has to beat.

## Open questions

1. Do any of the six enthusiastic participants also have an existing workaround, spreadsheet or
   otherwise? This directly changes the real disconfirmation rate. — owner: next interview round.
2. For the two disconfirmers: what would the product have to do that the spreadsheet cannot, at what
   price, before they would reconsider? Not asked this round. — owner: `customer-discovery-interviewer`.
3. Was "liked the idea" probed with a behavioural follow-up ("tell me about the last time you tried to
   solve this"), or only recorded as a reaction? The report as given cannot distinguish these. —
   owner: interview-guide review before the next round.
